Title & one-liner

Bauhaus Construct — Snap primary-color shapes onto a grid until they form the target composition.

Anchors

Gameplay like: Tetris — you place pieces one at a time until the board resolves into a complete composition. The tension is between the urgency of filling space and the precision required to hit exact positions; you can only rotate and place, never remove once locked, so every decision is permanent.

Visuals like: PixelHunters — flat 2D SVG canvas. Art direction is strictly Bauhaus Digital: primary-color planes (red, yellow, blue) and black/white ground and lines on a measured grid. No gradients, no shadows, no perspective. The grid itself is the aesthetic.

Sounds like: The Grain Snap — the generative music instrument from the foundry serves as underscore. Each piece placement triggers a single note from a pentatonic scale tied to the piece's color; as more pieces lock, rhythmic density increases; the final lock plays a resolved chord. Silence returns briefly between levels.

Core loop (first 30 seconds)

The player opens the page. A white canvas fills the screen, split in two: the top half shows a small target composition in primary colors on a subtle black grid; the bottom half shows a tray of 3–5 primitive shapes — a red square, a yellow rectangle, a blue circle, a black line — arranged in a row.

The first ten seconds: the target is visible but abstract. A black arrow or outline highlights the first empty space on the grid. The player clicks a piece from the tray to select it. The piece lifts, follows the cursor.

The player moves the piece over the grid. As it nears a valid cell, the grid cell highlights in a faint black outline. When the piece's footprint aligns with the grid, it snaps audibly — a short percussive tone from the generative instrument.

Three verbs: select (click a piece from the tray), move (drag it over the grid), snap (it locks into place with a note). That is the entire loop, repeated: select, move, snap. Repeat until the composition is complete.

Every row is implementable: grid is SVG <g> with <rect> cells; pieces are SVG <rect>, <circle>, <line>. Drag via pointer events. Snap via nearest-grid-cell distance. Audio via Web Audio API oscillator nodes mapped to piece colors.

Win / Lose

Win: All required cells in the target composition are filled by locked pieces. The composition resolves: a 600 ms hold of the completed composition with a final resolved chord (all piece-color notes together, fading to white). "New" button appears.

Lose: No pieces remain on the tray and the composition is incomplete. Grid outline pulses red; locked pieces remain; tray shows empty. "Retry" button appears.

Run length: 30–90 seconds per level. Early levels: 3–5 pieces. Later levels: up to 8–10 pieces with more constrained shapes.

Session shape

  • First 10 seconds: Target top-half, tray bottom-half. Animated black arrow sweeps in one direction indicating the first empty cell. Player drags the first piece in — no text. Grid snaps the piece with sound. By second 10, the player has locked one piece and feels the mechanic.
  • First minute: Player places 2–3 pieces. Each snap adds a note to an emerging rhythm. The composition starts to feel familiar — player recognizes the grid as workspace, tray as palette. Second lock triggers: "I am building this."
  • Replay hook: Tight loop, no penalty, instant retry. Satisfaction of pieces clicking into place. The generative music creates a personal soundtrack the player wants to hear complete again.

Difficulty ramp

What escalates: number of pieces (3 → 10), grid complexity (simple blocks → irregular shapes), and available rotations (1 → 4 orientations). Curve is linear — each level adds one piece or one shape variation. The player earns a time bonus for completing levels with fewer moves; no purchases, no power-ups, no micro-economy.

Why it's fun (falsifiable hypothesis)

Precision vs. greed. The player wants to place pieces quickly and celebrate early completion, but placing a piece in the wrong cell makes a level unwinnable (pieces lock permanently, no undo). The satisfying moment comes from resisting the urge to lock prematurely and thinking one step ahead.

Confirm if: Majority of failed levels occur because a player locked an early piece that blocked a later one. Observation: players hover over the grid longer than average placement time on later pieces; players click a second time before the first piece fully locks, indicating regret.

Refute if: Players fail because the level layout is genuinely unsolvable (no valid arrangement fits the target). If players complete levels in under 5 seconds with no hesitation, the precision requirement is absent and the tension is not present.

Scope budget — the OUT list

This game explicitly does NOT have in v1:

  • 3D, perspective, or WebGL. Everything is flat 2D SVG on a grid.
  • Photorealism. Shapes are geometric primitives: squares, rectangles, circles, lines, triangles. No textures, no photos, no gradients.
  • Extra colors beyond the primary palette. Red (#E04040), yellow (#F0C830), blue (#3060D0), black (#1A1A1A), white (#F5F5F0). No green, orange, purple, or any other hue in v1.
  • Narrative or story. No characters, no plot, no dialogue. No flavor text explaining the game.
  • Power-ups, collectibles, or items. Pure composition.
  • Multiplayer or networking. Single-player only. No leaderboards in v1.
  • Save/load. No persistence. Refreshing resets everything.
  • In-app purchases, ads, monetization.
  • Sound effects other than generative music. No UI beeps, explosions, or ambient noise.
  • Menus beyond level select and retry. No settings screen, no options menu.
  • Touch swiping. Only click/tap and drag. No gesture controls.
  • Variable piece sizes beyond the grid. All pieces align to the grid exactly.

Test API

The browser game must expose window.__GAME as a namespace:

State queries (read-only)

  • getState() — returns { level: number, locked: Array<{cell: {row, col}, color: string, rotation: number}>, tray: Array<{type, color, rotation}>, target: Array<{row, col, color}>, phase: 'idle'|'playing'|'win'|'lose' }
  • canPlace(piece, row, col, rotation) — boolean: whether placing piece at row, col with rotation is valid (not overlapping, within bounds).
  • isLevelComplete() — boolean.
  • getAvailablePieces() — array of pieces still on tray.
  • isLevelSolvable() — boolean (backtracking solver for critic validation).

Simulation hooks (mutating)

  • placePiece(pieceIndex, row, col, rotation) — simulates locking a piece. Returns { success, note }.
  • rotatePiece(pieceIndex, degrees) — rotates a tray piece.
  • deselect() — clears currently selected piece.
  • nextLevel() — advances, resets state.
  • retryLevel() — resets current level to initial state.
  • resetAll() — resets everything to level 1.
  • simulateWin() — forces win state for testing resolution animation.
  • simulateLose() — forces lose state (removes tray pieces, leaves locked) for testing lose render.

Critic test scenarios

  1. getState().phase === 'playing' after init — game started correctly.
  2. canPlace(0, 2, 3, 0) returns true — valid placement recognized.
  3. canPlace(0, 2, 3, 0) then placePiece(0, 2, 3, 0) — afterward, getState().locked contains the piece.
  4. canPlace(0, 2, 3, 0) returns false after placement — piece now locked.
  5. isLevelComplete() returns true after all target cells filled — win detection works.
  6. isLevelSolvable() returns true for all generated levels — no unsolvable levels.
  7. simulateWin() — getState().phase becomes 'win' — win animation triggers.
  8. simulateLose() — getState().phase becomes 'lose' — lose state renders.
  9. All audio uses Web Audio API (no <audio> tags) — generative music integrated.
